# The US-Japan income tax treaty and the re-sourcing rule

```
As of:        2026-07-28
Applies to:   The 2003 Convention as amended by the 2013 Protocol, in force from
              30 August 2019
Verified by:  Convention text read verbatim from sources/treaty/us-japan-treaty-2003.pdf
```

## 1. What the treaty does not do

**Article 1(4)(a) — the saving clause:**

> Except to the extent provided in paragraph 5, this Convention **shall not affect
> the taxation by a Contracting State of its residents** (as determined under
> Article 4) **and, in the case of the United States, its citizens**.

The US therefore continues to tax the user on worldwide income exactly as if the
treaty did not exist. **No treaty article reduces the US tax** on a US citizen
unless it appears in the Article 1(5) exception list.

## 2. What survives the saving clause — Article 1(5)

> The provisions of paragraph 4 shall not affect the benefits conferred by a
> Contracting State under paragraphs 2 and 3 of Article 9, paragraph 3 of Article
> 17, and Articles 18, 19, 20, **23**, 24, 25 and 28 ...

**Article 23 — Relief from Double Taxation — is on the list.** This is the pivotal
fact in the document: the relief article, including its re-sourcing rule, **is
available to a US citizen resident in Japan** notwithstanding the saving clause.

[Convention arts. 1(4), 1(5)] — `sources/treaty/us-japan-treaty-2003.pdf`

## 3. Article 13 — who may tax gains

> **7.** Gains from the alienation of any property other than that referred to in
> the preceding paragraphs of this Article shall be **taxable only in the
> Contracting State of which the alienator is a resident**.

The enumerated exceptions in paragraphs 1-6 are real property (¶1), shares in
real-property-rich companies deriving ≥50% of value from real property in the other
state (¶2(a)), partnership/trust interests to the extent of such real property
(¶2(b)), a rescued-financial-institution rule (¶3), permanent establishment
business property (¶4), ships and aircraft in international traffic (¶5), and
containers (¶6).

**Ordinary listed securities are not among them.** So under ¶7, gains on the user's
portfolio are **taxable only in Japan** once they are a Japanese resident — as a
treaty matter. The saving clause then lets the US tax them anyway, and Article 23
is what stops that becoming straightforward double taxation.

Note ¶2(a)'s carve-out: even for a real-property-rich company, gains escape if the
class of shares is traded on a recognised exchange and the holder's group owns
**5% or less** of the class.

## 4. Article 23 — the relief machinery

### Article 23(2) — the US credit

> In accordance with the provisions and subject to the limitations of the laws of
> the United States ... the United States shall allow to a **resident or citizen**
> of the United States as a credit against the United States tax on income: (a) the
> Japanese tax paid or accrued by or on behalf of such citizen or resident ...

and, for **US residents**:

> an item of gross income, as determined under the laws of the United States,
> derived by a resident of the United States that, under this Convention, may be
> taxed in Japan **shall be deemed to be income from sources in Japan**.

That last sentence is limited to a *resident* of the United States. A US citizen
who is a Japanese resident under Article 4 is not covered by it — which is why
paragraph 3 exists.

### Article 23(3) — the citizen mechanism

Where the US taxes, under Article 1(4), a **citizen** who is a **resident of Japan**:

> **(a)** Japan shall take into account for the purposes of computing the credit to
> be allowed under paragraph 1 **only the amount of tax that the United States may
> impose** on income under the provisions of this Convention that is derived by a
> resident of Japan who is **neither a citizen, nor a former citizen nor long-term
> resident**, of the United States;
>
> **(b)** for the purposes of computing the United States tax on income referred to
> in subparagraph (a), the United States shall allow as a credit against the United
> States tax **the Japanese tax after the credit referred to in that subparagraph**;
> the credit so allowed shall not reduce the portion of the United States tax that
> is creditable against the Japanese tax in accordance with that subparagraph; and
>
> **(c)** for the exclusive purpose of allowing the credit by the United States
> provided for under subparagraph (b), income referred to in subparagraph (a)
> **shall be deemed to arise in Japan to the extent necessary to allow the United
> States to grant the credit** provided for in subparagraph (b).

[Convention art. 23(3)] — `sources/treaty/us-japan-treaty-2003.pdf`

### How this resolves the capital gains problem

Trace a gain on the pre-arrival portfolio, sold while a Japanese resident and taxed
by Japan:

1. **Article 13(7)**: taxable only in Japan. The US could impose **nothing** on a
   non-citizen Japanese resident.
2. **Article 23(3)(a)**: Japan therefore credits **nothing** against Japanese tax —
   Japan taxes in full and gives no relief.
3. **Article 23(3)(b)**: the **US** must allow a credit for the Japanese tax. The US
   is the country that gives way.
4. **Article 23(3)(c)**: the income is **deemed to arise in Japan** to the extent
   needed to make that credit work — supplying §904 limitation room that US
   domestic sourcing might not.
5. **§904(d)(6)** then applies the limitation **separately to each re-sourced item**
   (doc 06 §3).

**Article 23 fixes the ordering, and it supplies re-sourcing.** But note it is a
**belt-and-braces** fix here rather than the primary one: as doc 05 §1 shows, once
Japan actually taxes the gain at 20.315%, **§865(g)(2) is satisfied domestically**
and the gain is already foreign-source under US domestic law. Article 23(3)(c)
matters where domestic sourcing falls short — for example where Japanese tax on a
particular sale is below 10% of the gain because the remittance ordering rule
brought in only part of it (doc 05 Open question 4).

**What the treaty does not fix:** it does not reduce US tax below the credit, it
does not help where the §904 limitation is exhausted, and it does not reach NIIT on
the mainstream reading.

### Form 8833

Taking a treaty-based return position generally requires disclosure on **Form 8833**
under §6114, with a penalty for failure. Whether re-sourcing under Article 23(3)
requires disclosure, and in what circumstances, is **not established** here — see
Open questions.

## 5. Other articles

- **Article 4 (Residence)** — a US citizen living in Japan is generally a treaty
  resident of Japan. The tie-breaker cascade (permanent home → centre of vital
  interests → habitual abode → nationality → competent authority) resolves dual
  residence, relevant in the arrival and departure years.
- **Article 14 (Income from Employment)** — the short-stay exemption. Not
  transcribed here; relevant to the pre-residency phase where work is performed in
  Japan before residency begins. **This is a gap** — see Open questions, because
  doc 09's pre-arrival lever may involve exactly such visits.
- **Articles 10 and 11 (Dividends, Interest)** — reduced withholding rates. The
  2013 Protocol notably moved much interest to source-country exemption. Not
  transcribed; affects the credit arithmetic on portfolio income.
- **Article 25** — mutual agreement procedure, with arbitration added by the 2013
  Protocol. The backstop where the two administrations disagree.
- **Social security** — the **US-Japan Totalization Agreement** determines which
  system the user contributes to and prevents dual coverage. **Not archived here.**
  Japanese social insurance premiums are **not** creditable as income taxes
  (doc 06 §1); whether they are deductible is not established.

## 6. Confidence

**Settled** — quoted verbatim from the archived Convention:

- The Article 1(4) saving clause and its application to US citizens.
- **That Article 23 is listed in Article 1(5) and so survives the saving clause.**
  This is the load-bearing fact of the document.
- Article 13(7): gains on property other than the enumerated categories are taxable
  only in the state of residence, with listed securities not enumerated.
- Article 13(2)(a)'s real-property-rich company rule and its 5%/listed carve-out.
- The full text and operation of Article 23(2) and 23(3)(a)-(c), including the
  re-sourcing rule in (c).

**Likely but unverified:**

- That the 2013 Protocol did not amend Articles 1, 13 or 23 in ways that change the
  above. The Protocol **is archived** [`us-japan-protocol-2013.pdf`] but has **not
  been read against** the 2003 text. **This is a real gap** — the quotations above
  come from the 2003 Convention as signed, not from a consolidated text.
- The Article 4 tie-breaker cascade wording.
- That Form 8833 disclosure is required for Article 23(3) re-sourcing.

**Open questions:**

1. **Read the 2013 Protocol against the 2003 Convention** and produce a
   consolidated text of Articles 1, 4, 13 and 23. Everything in this document
   depends on the 2003 wording still being current. **Do this before the calculator
   relies on Article 23.**
2. **Transcribe Article 14** and the short-stay exemption conditions.
3. **Transcribe Articles 10 and 11** withholding rates as amended by the Protocol.
4. **Archive the Totalization Agreement** and establish the contribution position
   and the deductibility of Japanese social insurance premiums — these are large
   recurring amounts.
5. **Whether Article 23(3)(c) re-sourcing is capped** by the tax Japan could impose
   under the Convention, and how that cap interacts with §904(d)(6)'s per-item
   limitation.
