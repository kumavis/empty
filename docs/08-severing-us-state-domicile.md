# Leaving US state tax domicile

Federal citizenship-based taxation follows the user to Japan regardless. **State
tax does not** — but only if the state's domicile link is genuinely broken. Get
this wrong and a state taxes worldwide income, **including capital gains**, with
**no credit for Japanese tax and no treaty protection**. That can exceed every
Japanese lever in doc 09 combined, and it is entirely avoidable.

## 1. Three different concepts called "domicile"

The user's phrase "transitioning from US domicile" blurs three legal concepts that
govern different things. Disentangling them is the main purpose of this document.

| Concept | Governs | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **State domicile** | State income tax | Common-law domicile: physical presence + intent to remain, persisting until a new domicile is established |
| **Federal tax home** (§911(d)(3)) | §911 exclusion, **and §865 sourcing of gains** | Location of the principal place of business; defeated if the "abode" remains in the US |
| **Japanese domicile** (住所) | Japanese residency and the phases in doc 01 | Centre of one's life (生活の本拠), with the presumptions in Enforcement Order art. 14 |

They can diverge. Someone can be Japanese-domiciled from arrival (doc 01 §3), have
a federal tax home in Japan, and **still be domiciled in California** — paying
California tax on the very capital gains the Japanese remittance basis shelters.

**The federal tax home is the one that quietly matters most**, because §865(g)(1)
routes the sourcing of capital gains through it (doc 05 §1). A tax home that fails
to move keeps gains US-source.

## 2. The two independent bases of state taxation

A state can tax as a resident on **either** basis:

1. **Domicile** — the common-law concept. An existing domicile persists until a new
   one is established by **both** physical presence **and** intent. Moving abroad
   without forming an intent to remain does not shed it.
2. **Statutory residence** — a mechanical day-count plus a permanent place of abode,
   independent of domicile.

Leaving requires defeating both.

## 3. California

California FTB Publication 1031 [`sources/us-state/ca-ftb-pub1031.pdf`] states the
governing principle:

> Any individual who is a resident of California **continues to be a resident when
> absent from the state for a temporary or transitory purpose**.

So the question is whether the absence is "temporary or transitory" — a
facts-and-circumstances test weighing the closest-connection factors the FTB
publishes.

### The employment safe harbor

Pub 1031 provides a safe harbor:

> An absence from California under an **employment-related contract** for a period
> of **at least 546 consecutive days** may [qualify the individual as a nonresident].

and extends it to a spouse or registered domestic partner accompanying the
individual for the same period. Two limits appear in the publication's own worked
example:

- The 546 days must be an **uninterrupted period**. The example describes a
  taxpayer whose contract expired, who stayed three months, then signed a new
  contract — and who **could not** use the safe harbor, because periods **cannot be
  combined**.
- The publication elsewhere notes the safe harbor does not apply where the purpose
  is **to avoid personal income tax**.

546 days is roughly 18 months, so a genuine multi-year posting to Japan clears it —
provided the contract runs continuously and the return visits stay within the
publication's limits (which are stated in Pub 1031 but **not transcribed here**).

## 4. New York

New York is the other notoriously difficult state, taxing on **both** domicile and a
**statutory residence** test built on maintaining a permanent place of abode plus a
day count. There are also day-count safe harbors in the New York regulations for
taxpayers in foreign countries.

**None of this is verified.** The Form IT-203-I instructions are archived
[`sources/us-state/ny-it203i-instructions.pdf`] but have **not been read**. Do not
rely on any New York statement in this document.

## 5. No foreign tax credit at state level

States generally give **no credit for foreign income taxes** and **do not follow US
tax treaties** — treaties bind the federal government, not the states. A resident
of a taxing state pays state tax on Japanese income **on top of** Japanese tax, with
no relief mechanism at all.

This is the reason severing domicile matters so much more than its dull reputation
suggests. **Not verified for California or New York specifically** — see Open
questions.

## 6. Evidence of a change of domicile

States weigh published factor lists. The evidence that generally supports a change
includes: disposing of or letting the former residence; registering to vote in the
new place; moving driving licences and vehicle registrations; relocating bank and
brokerage accounts; moving family and household goods; professional and social ties;
and the location of the taxpayer's "near and dear" possessions.

*The general shape of these factors is standard; the specific lists in Pub 1031 and
the New York guidance are **not transcribed here**.*

## 7. Expatriation is out of scope

**IRC §877A** (the "exit tax") applies only on **renouncing US citizenship** or
abandoning long-term permanent residence. The user has not indicated any such
intention, so it is **out of scope**. Noted only to mark the boundary: nothing in
this repository's plan triggers §877A, and the Japanese exit tax in doc 04 is an
entirely separate charge that shares only a nickname.

## 8. Confidence

**Settled** — quoted from the archived FTB publication:

- California residents remain residents while absent for a "temporary or transitory
  purpose".
- The **546 consecutive day** employment-contract safe harbor, its extension to a
  spouse/RDP, that periods **cannot be combined**, and that it is unavailable where
  the purpose is tax avoidance.

**Likely but unverified:**

- The domicile/statutory-residence two-basis framework. Standard US state tax law,
  but not cited to a source here.
- That states give no foreign tax credit and do not follow treaties. Near-universal
  and structurally sound, but not verified for any specific state.
- The domicile factor list in §6.

**Open questions:**

1. **Read FTB Pub 1031 properly.** It is archived and unread beyond the two
   passages quoted. It contains the return-visit limits, the closest-connection
   factors, and the part-year return rules — all directly relevant.
2. **Read the New York instructions**, or establish that New York is irrelevant to
   this taxpayer. Which state is actually in play is a taxpayer-specific input that
   has not been supplied.
3. **Verify the no-foreign-credit point** in the specific state's own law.
4. **Part-year returns in the year of the move** — how gains realised after
   departure but in the same calendar year are treated. This interacts directly
   with doc 09's Lever 2.
5. **Whether an interim domicile in a no-income-tax state** is worth the complexity.
   It requires establishing genuine domicile there, not merely passing through.
