This document tests the user's own hypotheses against the law, then sets out the

## 1. The two hypotheses, tested

### Hypothesis (b): "Sending money to Japan on foreign capital gains during the non-permanent resident phase is taxed, while untransmitted gains are not."

**Correct in direction, but the mechanism is different and matters.**

What is right: a non-permanent resident (非永住者) is taxed on foreign-source income
only to the extent it is *paid within Japan* or *remitted from abroad*
[ITA art. 7(1)(ii), doc 02 §1].

What the phrasing misses, in three ways:

1. **It is a deeming rule, not a tracing rule.** You cannot designate *which*
   money you sent. Enforcement Order art. 17(4)(i) deems any remittance to carry
   the year's foreign-source income, up to the lesser of the remittance and that
   income [doc 02 §4]. Sending "savings, not gains" is not a thing the law
   recognises.
2. **The exposure is capped by the year's income, and stacked favourably.** The
   remittance is deemed to come out of *non*-foreign-source income first. Only the
   excess reaches sheltered gains. So the practical rule is: **keep annual
   remittances at or below your Japan-source income paid abroad** and the shelter
   holds intact.
3. **Not all gains are shelterable.** Only *specified securities* (特定有価証券) —
   those **acquired outside the non-permanent resident period** — are foreign-source
   at all [Order art. 17(1), doc 02 §3]. Anything **bought after arrival** is taxed
   on an arising basis whether or not it is remitted.

### Hypothesis (a): "It may be optimal to send significant funds before residence, so as to earn but not transmit capital gains during residence."

**Confirmed, and for a stronger reason than stated** — but its *value* is much
smaller than it appears, because of the US side.

The reason it works is not that pre-arrival money is traceable. It is that
**Enforcement Order art. 17(4)(vi) applies the remittance rules only to income
arising and remittances received *during* the non-permanent resident period**
[doc 02 §4]. Money moved before residency begins is not a remittance under the
rule at all. It is outside the regime, not favourably treated within it.

That also means the deadline is the **residency start date, not 1 January**. A
transfer made in the same calendar year but before the day domicile attaches is
still pre-residency. Since domicile normally attaches on arrival [doc 01 §3], the
window closes on the day of the move.

**But now the US side.** Doc 05 §1 shows §865(g)(2) sources a citizen's gains
abroad *only if at least 10% foreign tax is actually paid on that gain*. So:

| | Japan | US | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gain sheltered (not remitted) | 0 | full US tax, gain is US-source | **full US tax** |
| Gain remitted / phase 3 | 20.315% | credit, subject to §904 limits | **≈ higher of the two, plus uncreditable NIIT** |

> **The remittance shelter saves Japanese tax. It does not save US tax.** A US
> citizen who models "unremitted gains are untaxed" is wrong by the entire US
> liability. This is the most important correction in this repository, and it is
> the point most likely to be missed, because almost all English-language writing
> on the Japanese remittance basis is aimed at non-Americans, for whom the shelter
> really is close to absolute.

The shelter is still worth real money, because the US credit does **not** fully
neutralise the Japanese tax:

- **NIIT (3.8%)** is uncreditable on the mainstream reading [doc 05 §3].
- The **§904(b)(2)(B) capital gain rate differential adjustment** scales down the
  limitation numerator on preferential-rate gains, so part of the Japanese
  20.315% is typically not creditable in-year [doc 06 §5].
- **Excess general-basket credits from salary cannot relieve passive-basket tax on
  gains** [doc 06 §3]. Being drowned in unused salary credits does not help.

So the saving from non-remittance is roughly *the non-creditable portion of the
Japanese 20.315%* — not the whole 20.315%, and not zero. Where the US long-term
rate is 15% rather than 20%, the saving is larger.

## 2. The levers

### Lever 1 — Fund Japanese living costs before residency begins

**Move the money you expect to spend in Japan before the day domicile attaches.**
Pre-residency transfers are outside the remittance regime entirely
[Order art. 17(4)(vi)]. Every yen pre-positioned is a yen that never has to be
remitted during a year when a remittance would drag in sheltered gains.

Size it against expected Japanese living costs for the whole non-permanent resident
phase — potentially five years — net of Japanese salary, since salary paid in Japan
covers costs without any remittance at all.

**Costs to weigh honestly:**
- Currency risk: converting five years of expenses to yen up front is an unhedged
  FX bet, and a large adverse move can dwarf the tax saved.
- Yen cash earns little; the opportunity cost against invested dollars is real.
- Japanese banks are hard to open accounts with **before** you have a residence
  card, which may force the transfer to a pre-existing account or delay it past the
  deadline. **Sequence this carefully** — the lever expires on arrival day.

### Lever 2 — Realise gains before residency, or hold them past it

Three windows for a pre-arrival portfolio:

| When realised | Japan | US |
|---|---|---|
| **Before residency** | Nothing — non-resident, foreign gain | Full US tax |
| **During non-permanent residence, unremitted** | Nothing (specified securities) | Full US tax, US-source |
| **During non-permanent residence, remitted** | Up to 20.315% on the deemed-remitted portion | US tax with credit |
| **Phase 3 (worldwide)** | 20.315% in full | US tax with credit |

The first two rows are **identical in outcome**. So realising before the move buys
nothing over realising during the sheltered phase — which means **there is no rush
to sell before arriving**. That is a useful negative result: it removes pressure to
liquidate on a deadline and lets the decision be driven by investment merit.

What *is* time-limited is the **end** of phase 2. After it, every realisation costs
20.315% in Japan. If large embedded gains exist that will eventually be realised,
the five-year boundary is a hard deadline to realise them against
[doc 01 §4].

> **Verify before relying on this:** Japan taxes gains on **original cost basis**.
> There is no step-up to market value on becoming a Japanese resident. A position
> held for twenty years brings its entire lifetime gain into the Japanese net once
> phase 3 starts. **This is stated on the strength of the absence of any step-up
> provision in the archived statutes, not on a positive citation — see §4.**

### Lever 3 — Never buy securities into the shelter

Enforcement Order art. 17(1) shelters only securities **acquired outside** the
non-permanent resident period. Anything purchased after arrival is taxed on an
arising basis, remitted or not.

Practical consequences:
- **Do not rebalance a sheltered portfolio during phase 2** if it means selling and
  repurchasing — the repurchased lot loses the shelter permanently.
- The forced **FIFO** rule (art. 17(2)) means earliest lots are deemed sold first,
  so pre-arrival lots are consumed before post-arrival ones. Favourable while not
  remitting; it also means the shelter is depleted by selling, in a fixed order you
  cannot choose.
- Corporate actions preserve the acquisition date (art. 17(3)), so a merger or
  split does not silently destroy the shelter.
- **Do not buy Japanese mutual funds or investment trusts** — they are almost
  certainly PFICs for US purposes [doc 05 §4], which is a far worse problem than
  anything else here.

### Lever 4 — Remittance discipline during phase 2

The operating rule from doc 02 §4:

```
taxable foreign-source income  =  max(0, min(remittance, FSI_abroad + nonFSI_abroad) − nonFSI_abroad)
```

where `FSI_abroad` is the year's foreign-source income paid abroad and
`nonFSI_abroad` is the year's non-foreign-source income paid abroad, both measured
under art. 17(4)(ii) — **employment income gross, capital gains net**.

Implications:
- **Realise gains in years you do not remit.** The rule is annual and resets.
- **A year with no foreign-source income is a free remittance year.** Move capital
  in a year with no realisations.
- Being paid **in Japan** for Japanese work is helpful: it funds living costs
  without any remittance, so the shelter is never touched.
- Watch bucket B — foreign dividends paid **into a Japanese account** are taxed
  regardless of remittance [doc 02 §2]. Keep foreign income flowing to foreign
  accounts.

### Lever 5 — Do not take immigration Permanent Residence casually

The exit tax (国外転出時課税) excludes time spent under an Immigration **Appended
Table 1** status — ordinary work visas — from its five-year clock
[Order art. 170(3)(i), doc 04 §1]. A work-visa holder is **never** exposed,
regardless of portfolio size or length of stay.

Switching to a **Table 2** status — Permanent Resident, Spouse of a Japanese
National — **starts the clock**. Five years later, departure with ¥100m+ of
securities triggers a deemed disposal of the whole portfolio.

> Immigration Permanent Residence is presented as a convenience upgrade. For a
> large portfolio it silently arms a deemed-disposal charge. **Treat it as a tax
> decision.** Note this is independent of the income-tax phases: the exit tax clock
> and the non-permanent resident clock count different things.

### Lever 6 — Departure timing

**Leave in December, not January.** Inhabitant tax is keyed to residence on
**1 January** and assessed on the *prior* year's income [doc 03 §3]. Departing
before 1 January avoids a bill of roughly **10% of a full year's income**. It costs
nothing but a calendar choice, and it is probably the single largest one-off saving
available. *(Rests on the unverified inhabitant tax rules — confirm first.)*

Appoint a **tax agent** (納税管理人) before leaving: it preserves the filing
timetable and secures the departure-date valuation if the exit tax ever applies
[doc 04 §1].

**Moving funds out is not itself taxable** — art. 7's rule is inbound-only
[doc 04 §3]. The residual costs are FX gain and reporting.

### Lever 7 — Sever US state domicile properly

See doc 08. A state that still considers you domiciled will tax worldwide income
including capital gains, and **states do not give credit for foreign taxes and do
not follow treaties**. This can cost more than every Japanese lever combined and is
entirely avoidable.

### Lever 8 — Choose the credit over §911, and elect accrual

For a high earner facing Japanese rates near 55%, **§911 is usually the wrong
election** — it burns creditable Japanese tax to exclude income the credit would
have covered anyway, while §911(f) stacking keeps the marginal rate [doc 06 §7].
Model both.

Elect the **accrued** basis for foreign taxes in year one, to match inhabitant tax
to the income it relates to rather than lagging a year [doc 06 §6].

## 3. Earned income versus capital gains — the comparison

| | Earned income | Capital gains (pre-arrival securities) |
|---|---|---|
| **Japan rate** | Progressive to 45% + 2.1% surtax + ~10% local ≈ **55%** | **20.315%** flat |
| **Japan mode** | Aggregate (総合課税) | Separate self-assessment (申告分離課税) |
| **Japan source** | Where services performed → Japan-source if working in Japan | Foreign, if specified securities |
| **Shelterable by non-remittance?** | **No**, if work is performed in Japan | **Yes** |
| **Measured for remittance as** | **Gross** receipts (art. 17(4)(ii)) | **Net** gain |
| **US exclusion available?** | Yes, §911, ~$130k | **No** |
| **US source** | Foreign (§861(a)(3)) | Depends on §865(g)(2) → on whether Japan taxed it |
| **US FTC basket** | General | Passive |
| **US rate** | To 37% | 0/15/20% + 3.8% NIIT |
| **Credit works well?** | Yes — usually excess credits | **Poorly** — §904(b)(2)(B) scaling, NIIT uncreditable |

**The two categories behave in opposite directions.** Earned income is taxed
brutally by Japan but the US credit relieves it cleanly, so the US adds nothing.
Capital gains are taxed lightly by Japan and can be sheltered entirely — but the US
tax is owed regardless, and the credit works *badly* precisely where you need it.

The practical consequence: **optimise earned income for the Japanese rate, and
capital gains for the US rate.** They are different problems.

## 4. Where this analysis could be wrong

1. **The no-step-up assumption (Lever 2).** Asserted from the *absence* of a
   step-up provision, not a positive citation. If Japan did allow a market-value
   basis on becoming resident, the phase-2 deadline would matter far less.
   **Highest-value item to verify.**
2. **The §865(g)(2) interlock may split within a year** (doc 05 Open question 4).
   If the 10% test is applied sale-by-sale and the ordering rule taxes only part of
   a year's gains, some sales are foreign-source and others US-source. The
   calculator's arithmetic depends on this.
3. **Inhabitant tax creditability** (doc 06) and the **inhabitant tax rules
   themselves** (doc 03) are unverified. Levers 6 and 8 rest on them.
4. **The 2013 Protocol has not been read against the 2003 Convention** (doc 07).
   Article 23 quotations are from the 2003 text.
5. **The high-tax kickout** could reclassify Japanese-taxed gains out of the passive
   basket, changing doc 06 §3's conclusions (doc 06 Open questions).
6. **Any Japanese nationality destroys the plan.** A dual national cannot be a
   non-permanent resident [doc 01 §2]. Confirm first.
7. **Prior Japanese presence shortens phase 2.** Any time in Japan within ten years
   of arrival counts toward the five years.
8. **What counts as a remittance** is unresolved (doc 02 §6). If foreign card and
   ATM use are remittances, ordinary living expenditure silently erodes the shelter.

## 5. Inputs the calculator needs

Derived from the levers above; this is the specification for the input model.

**Identity and status**
- Japanese nationality held? (destroys non-permanent resident status)
- US state of former domicile, and date domicile severed
- Filing status; dependants
- Immigration status, and any change from Appended Table 1 to Table 2, with dates

**Timeline**
- Date domicile attaches in Japan (residency start)
- Any prior Japanese presence within the preceding 10 years, with dates
- Planned departure date
- Derived: phase 2 end date; exit tax clock start (Table 2 only)

**Income, per year**
- Employment income, split by where services were performed, and by paid-in-Japan
  versus paid-abroad
- Other Japan-source income
- Foreign dividends and interest, split by paid-in-Japan versus paid-abroad

**Assets and realisations**
- Per lot: acquisition date, cost basis, currency, whether held at a foreign broker
  — needed for both the specified-securities test and FIFO
- Planned realisations: date, lot, proceeds
- Total covered-asset value at departure (¥100m exit tax threshold)

**Cash movements**
- Remittances to Japan, per year, with dates (pre- versus post-residency)
- Pre-positioned funds moved before residency

**Elections**
- §911 elected?
- Foreign tax credit on paid or accrued basis?
- Treaty positions taken (Form 8833)

**Rates and constants** (versioned by tax year)
- Japanese brackets, deductions, surtax, inhabitant tax
- US brackets, capital gains rates, NIIT threshold, FEIE amount
- Exchange rates (average annual, per §986(a))
